How Australia Moves

Australians play more sport than almost anyone, yet only one in four adults moves enough to meet the national physical activity guidelines. This is where the gap sits.

85.9% active at least once a year
50.1% active 3+ times a week
23.9% meet the guidelines
600+ activities recorded

What Australians actually play

  • AusPlay records more than six hundred activities, but participation concentrates in a handful.
  • Most are not organised sport. Walking alone reaches half the adult population.

Five activities reach more than one adult in seven. The remaining hundreds share what is left.

Figure 1: Adult participation rate by activity, 2025. The scale is logarithmic because the spread is so wide.

A long tail

  • Walking, the gym, bush walking, running and swimming are the only activities above fifteen per cent, and every one can be done alone.
  • Team sports sit far down the scale: Australian football 2.4 per cent, cricket 2.5.

Does the order change from year to year?

  • Mostly it does not — the eight biggest activities held the same positions in both years.
  • The movement happens further down, where a fraction of a point swaps two activities.

Yoga overtook tennis and golf overtook basketball. Everything above them stayed put.

Figure 2: Rank of the twelve most popular activities, 2024 compared with 2025.

Only about one adult in four does enough activity to meet the national guidelines.

Figure 3: Out of every 100 Australian adults, how many meet the physical activity guidelines, 2022.

Where Australians are active

  • Half of all Australian adults are active at least three times a week.
  • That average hides a wide spread: eleven percentage points separate the top from the bottom.

Eleven and a half percentage points separate the most active jurisdiction from the least active one.

Figure 4: Adults active at least three times a week, by state, 2025.

Reading the map

  • The ACT leads at 59.2 per cent, almost twelve points clear of New South Wales on 47.9.
  • Both of the most populous states sit below the national figure of 50.1 per cent.
  • Hover any state for both years and the change.

Two more ways to look at the same country

  • Figure 5 replaces the rate with a headcount.
  • Figure 6 drops to more than two thousand small areas, where the capital cities separate from the country around them.

New South Wales and Victoria together account for more than half of all participants, even though neither reaches the national rate.

Figure 5: Number of adults who took part in sport or physical activity at least once in 2025, by state.

Grey is land with no small area in it. The population, and so the data, sits on the coast.

Figure 6: People meeting the physical activity guidelines, 2022, averaged across the small areas in each cell and weighted by their populations.

Rising or falling

  • Participation rose almost everywhere, Western Australia most at 4.2 points; Tasmania alone went backwards.
  • The sharpest movement was generational rather than geographic.

Teenagers aged 15 to 17 made the biggest jump of any age group, up 8.5 points in a single year.

Figure 7: Change in weekly participation by age group, 2024 to 2025.

More than a quarter of people aged over 75 did no physical activity at all in the week surveyed.

Figure 8: Minutes of physical activity in the last week, by age group, 2022.

Different activities, different ages

  • The age pattern is not the same for every activity.
  • Some climb steadily with age, others fall away almost completely.

Walking is the only one of the six that keeps growing with age. Football all but disappears after 45.

Figure 9: Participation in six major activities by age group, 2025. All panels share one scale.

The ACT stays on top at all three thresholds, and the drop-off from yearly to weekly participation is steep everywhere.

Figure 10: Each state at three levels of commitment, 2025, from taking part once a year to turning up three times a week.

Men, women and the years in between

  • Playing sport and moving enough for your health are not the same thing.
  • Against the guidelines the picture changes twice: between the sexes, and across the working years.

Men out-move women in every age group, and the widest gap of all is among 18 to 24 year olds.

Figure 11: Share of men and women meeting the physical activity guidelines, by age group, 2022.

The mid-life dip

  • Both sexes are least active between 45 and 64, when only about one in six meets the guidelines.
  • Among men aged 65 to 74 the share climbs back to 39.9 per cent, the highest of any group.

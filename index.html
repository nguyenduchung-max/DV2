How Australia Moves

Australians play more sport than almost anyone, yet only one in four adults moves enough to meet the national physical activity guidelines. This is where the gap sits.

85.9% active at least once a year
50.1% active 3+ times a week
23.9% meet the guidelines
600+ activities recorded

What Australians actually play

Before asking how much Australians move, it helps to know what they do. AusPlay records more than six hundred activities, but participation is concentrated in a handful of them, and most of those are not organised sport at all. Walking alone reaches half the adult population.

Five activities reach more than one adult in seven. The remaining hundreds share what is left.

Figure 1: Adult participation rate by activity, 2025. The scale is logarithmic because the spread is so wide.

A long tail

Walking, the gym, bush walking, running and swimming are the only activities reaching more than fifteen per cent of adults. Every one of them can be done alone, without a club, a season or a booking.

Traditional team sports sit far down the scale. Australian football reaches 2.4 per cent of adults and cricket 2.5 per cent, roughly one twentieth of walking.

Does the order change from year to year?

Mostly it does not. The eight biggest activities held exactly the same positions in 2024 and 2025, which is why the lines below run flat. The movement happens further down the list, where a fraction of a percentage point is enough to swap two activities around. The chart beside it puts all of this popularity in perspective: taking part at all is not the same as moving enough.

Yoga overtook tennis and golf overtook basketball. Everything above them stayed put.

Where Australians are active

Half of all Australian adults are active at least three times a week, but that national average hides a wide spread. The most active jurisdiction sits more than eleven percentage points above the least active one. Climate, distance to facilities and the mix of jobs in each state all pull in different directions, and the map below shows how far apart the ends of that range really are.

Eleven and a half percentage points separate the most active jurisdiction from the least active one.

Figure 4: Adults active at least three times a week, by state, 2025.

Reading the map

The Australian Capital Territory leads the country at 59.2 per cent, almost twelve points clear of New South Wales on 47.9 per cent. The two most populous states, New South Wales and Queensland, both sit below the national figure of 50.1 per cent, so the places with the most people are not the places with the most active people.

Hover over any state to see its 2024 rate, its 2025 rate and the change between them.

Rising or falling

Participation rose almost everywhere between 2024 and 2025. Western Australia gained the most ground of any state, up 4.2 points, while Tasmania was the only one to go backwards. The sharpest movement of all, though, was not geographic but generational, and it came from the youngest group in the survey.

Teenagers aged 15 to 17 made the biggest jump of any age group, up 8.5 points in a single year.

Figure 7: Change in weekly participation by age group, 2024 to 2025.

More than a quarter of people aged over 75 did no physical activity at all in the week surveyed.

Men, women and the years in between

Playing sport and moving enough for your health are not the same thing. Measured against the national guidelines rather than against participation, the picture changes twice: once between men and women, and once across the working years, when the share of people meeting the guidelines falls for two decades before recovering in retirement.

Men out-move women in every age group, and the widest gap of all is among 18 to 24 year olds.

Figure 11: Share of men and women meeting the physical activity guidelines, by age group, 2022.

The mid-life dip

Both men and women are at their least active between 45 and 64, when only about one in six meets the guidelines. The recovery after 65 is striking: among men aged 65 to 74 the share climbs back to 39.9 per cent, the highest of any group in the survey.

The gap between the sexes is widest in early adulthood, at almost eleven points, and narrows to nothing by the mid-fifties.
